[The last issue](/newsletter/three-hundred-followers/) ended with a test for the
week after it, and I called the test boring: keep the same cadence for seven
days with no spike in them, and see whether the floor moves.

This is that week. What I found out is what cadence costs once it is multiplied
by four.

## What happened

After launch, every platform turned into a to-do list. Instagram, TikTok, X,
LinkedIn, and a few more queued up behind them. Each one had a good reason to be
there, and each one, on its own, was a reasonable thing to take on. Together they
were not a marketing plan. They were a tide, and by this week I was underneath
it.

Nothing broke. There is no wreckage to point at, which is exactly the shape of
[my last setback](/newsletter/my-worst-setback/), and it is the reason I am
writing this one down while it is still happening instead of six weeks after.

## Four platforms is four jobs

The mistake is inside the phrase "post everywhere". It sounds like one task done
four times. It is four different jobs that happen to share a subject.

TikTok and Instagram want vertical video, cut to survive the first second. X
wants a sentence that stands on its own. LinkedIn wants a different voice
entirely. They reward different lengths, different times of day and different
ways of replying. A post that works on one is, at best, raw material for the
other three, and turning it into that raw material is a piece of work of its
own.

So what I thought was one new habit was four new jobs, each with its own
learning curve, all started in the same month.

## Marketing has no commit

In [A Hundred Commits](/newsletter/a-hundred-commits/) I wrote that a commit is
a place you are allowed to stop. That is what made a sixteen-hour day
survivable: every unit of work was small enough to close.

Marketing has no equivalent. A post is not finished when it is published. Then
come the replies, then the numbers, then the next post, which the numbers are
already arguing about. Nothing closes. Every item on the list produces the next
item as a side effect, and the list never gets shorter, it only gets rearranged.

And every item is a decision. What to post, where first, in which format,
whether to reply, whether yesterday's number meant anything. The same issue said
that deciding what to do next is the most expensive thing you can ask a tired
person to do. Marketing, done this way, is made of nothing else.

## It all happens on the phone

This is the part that makes it a setback rather than a busy week.

The last setback was measured in screen time: thirteen hours a day, for six
weeks. The work that has taken over this month lives on exactly the same device,
inside exactly the same apps, with the same feeds one swipe away from whatever I
opened them to do. An hour of marketing and an hour of drifting can look
identical, from the outside and sometimes from the inside.

That is not a reason to stop. It is a reason not to let it run unbounded,
because unbounded phone time is the one failure I have already been through and
already know the price of.

## Where the time comes from

Time spent on four feeds comes from somewhere, and the only place it can come
from is the building. Marketing is supposed to point at the product. If feeding
the feeds stops the product moving, then all of that work ends up pointing at
something standing still.

## What the fix has to look like

I have not solved it, and a setback piece that ends with a neat system would be
lying about the week. But the shape of the fix is not a mystery, because I
already know what worked the last time a day was too full.

1. **Decide the week in advance.** The same move that made the hundred-commit
   day possible. What goes out, where and in which format gets decided once, not
   live, forty times a day.
2. **One platform gets the original work.** The others get cut-downs of it, not
   originals of their own. The only one with any evidence behind it so far is
   X, a hundred thousand views in one week and three hundred followers after it,
   which is an argument for letting it lead.
3. **A box, not a tide.** Marketing gets a block of the day with an end to it.
   When the block ends, the phone goes down and the editor opens.
4. **Measure the input, again.** Screen time was visible weeks before I acted on
   it last time. This time it is the first number I look at, not the last.

This one is on [the timeline](/#timeline) as a setback, in the week it happened.
The last one I only named after six weeks. Naming this one inside its first week
is the only improvement I can actually prove so far.
